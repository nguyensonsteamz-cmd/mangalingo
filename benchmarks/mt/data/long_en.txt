The old lighthouse at the edge of the village had been abandoned for nearly thirty years, but the children still told stories about it. Some said a keeper's ghost climbed the stairs every night to light a lamp that no longer worked. Others insisted that the light was real, and that sailors who lost their way in the fog had been guided home by it more than once.

One autumn, a young engineer named Mai arrived to assess whether the building could be restored. She walked through the empty rooms with a notebook, measuring cracks in the walls and checking the rusted iron railing. "It is not as bad as it looks," she told the mayor. "The foundation is solid, and the tower can be saved if we reinforce the upper floors before winter."

The villagers were divided. Older fishermen wanted the lighthouse restored to guide boats, while the younger residents hoped to turn it into a small museum. After three long meetings, they agreed on a plan that would do both: the lamp would be repaired and lit every evening, and the ground floor would display photographs, maps and tools donated by local families.

Work began in the spring. Volunteers carried stones up the narrow path, and the school collected old letters and logbooks from the attic of every house. By midsummer, the tower had a new roof, and for the first time in decades a beam of light swept across the dark water. Nobody could say for certain whether the keeper's ghost approved, but the children noticed that the lamp never once flickered during the opening ceremony.
